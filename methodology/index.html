What NOSE calculates—and where it can be wrong.

The goal is a transparent comparison, not artificial certainty. This page describes the current prototype method in plain language.

  • Methodology and limitations

1. Read the available panel

NOSE uses terpene values included on a batch's Certificate of Analysis. Reports differ by laboratory and may omit compounds that still influence aroma.

NOSE compares 15 compounds, grouped into six aroma families:

  • Citrus: limonene
  • Earthy: myrcene
  • Spice: β-caryophyllene, α-humulene
  • Pine: α-pinene, β-pinene, fenchol, camphene
  • Floral: linalool, α-bisabolol, α-terpineol, nerolidol
  • Herbal: terpinolene, farnesene, ocimene

Anything else a lab measured is left out of the comparison. When you add a jar from a lab report, the card says how much of the report's terpene total those 15 cover.

Two reading rules apply to every report. A value below the lab's reporting limit, printed as ND, <LOQ or a less-than figure, counts as zero. cis- and trans-nerolidol are added together as one compound.

2. Normalize the profile

Each terpene is converted to its share of the measured terpene total. This emphasizes profile shape rather than total intensity.

shareᵢ = terpeneᵢ ÷ measured terpene total

A palate of several jars is the average of those jars' shares. Each jar counts equally, however strong it is.

3. Compare profile shape

The prototype uses cosine similarity across the shared terpene dimensions. A higher value means the measured proportions point in a more similar direction.

similarity = (A · B) ÷ (‖A‖ × ‖B‖)

4. Name the result

The number shown is the similarity times 100, rounded down, so a 74.9 shows 74. Each result carries one of four labels:

The four match labels and the score each starts at
LabelScore shown
Close match90 and up
Related profile75 to 89
Partial overlap55 to 74
Different profilebelow 55

These cut-offs are ones NOSE chose. They are not measured thresholds. The thumbs up and down on every result are how they get tested.

How often unrelated jars earn each label. Among the lab reports we test our reader with, two flower jars chosen without regard to each other score “Close match” about one time in four, and at least “Related profile” about two times in three. So a label tells you how two profiles compare; it does not mean the match is rare. That set of reports is small, and these figures will change as we test more.

5. Report confidence

The confidence label counts measured compounds. It is High when every jar compared, each jar in your palate and the new jar, lists at least nine of the 15. It is Moderate when the smallest count is five to eight, and Low below five. It is not a laboratory quality grade.

Important limitations

  • A COA is a measurement of a tested sample, not every flower in the container.
  • Terpene panels do not capture every volatile compound involved in aroma.
  • Storage, age, moisture, and packaging can change what reaches your nose.
  • Similarity does not predict medical benefit, impairment, or subjective effect. Whether it predicts enjoyment is still an open question, and the thumbs up and down on every result are there to test it.
