Why strain names drift

A strain name is a promise nobody is required to keep. Nothing ties a market name to one plant, and even the same plant can reach the jar differently from one harvest to the next. Here’s where the drift comes from, and what to trust instead.

  • 4 min read
  • 7 sources
  • Last reviewed September 2026

In short

  • No registry ties a strain name to a particular plant. A name can be printed on any jar.
  • In one genetic study of dispensary flower, only 4 of 30 named strains were consistent across all of their samples.
  • The indica, sativa and hybrid labels don’t line up with measured chemistry either.
  • Even identical genetics vary with where a bud grew on the plant, when it was harvested and how it was stored.
  • Labs measure different compound lists and can report systematically different numbers.
  • The report for your batch is the only description of your jar. Keep that, not just the name.

Nobody owns the name

There is no registry that ties a market strain name to a particular plant, and nothing stops two growers from selling different plants under the same name. Genetic researchers have described distinctive cannabis varieties as “nearly impossible to verify” for exactly this reason (Schwabe & McGlaughlin, 2019). In practice, a name is whatever the breeder, grower or shop decided to print.

The same name, different plants

When researchers bought 122 samples of 30 named strains from 20 dispensaries in three US states and compared their DNA, samples sharing a name often weren’t alike. Most strains had at least one genetic outlier, and only four of the 30 were consistent across every sample. All eight strains sold as “clone only,” meaning propagated from cuttings rather than seed, had at least one sample that didn’t match the rest (Schwabe & McGlaughlin, 2019).

There are ordinary reasons for this. A cross grown from seed produces a range of plants rather than copies, and each grower keeps the ones they like, so two growers can sell different plants under one name without anyone lying. Names can also be mislabeled or reused along the way.

The labels don’t hold either

The indica, sativa and hybrid labels printed beside names have the same problem. An analysis of 89,923 lab-tested samples from six US states found that those labels do not consistently align with the chemistry actually measured (Smith et al., 2022). A separate study genotyped more than 100 samples and found sativa- and indica-labeled plants genetically indistinct across the genome. What the labels did track was variation in a small number of terpenes (Watts et al., 2021).

So the chemistry is real and measurable. The 2022 analysis found samples falling into three main terpene patterns: one high in β-caryophyllene and limonene, one high in myrcene and pinene, and one high in terpinolene and myrcene. The names and labels just don’t reliably point to them. A lab report does.

Same plant, different jar

Even when the genetics match, the jar isn’t a fixed thing.

  • Where the bud grew. On a single plant, cannabinoid and terpene levels fall from the top of the flowering stem to the bottom (Namdar et al., 2018). A lab tests a sample; your jar holds whichever buds were packed.
  • When it was harvested. Clones of the same mother plants, grown under controlled conditions and harvested week by week, showed terpene levels changing through flowering, with monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes following different patterns (Aizpurua-Olaizola et al., 2016). Harvest earlier or later and the profile moves.
  • How it was stored. Terpenes evaporate and break down after harvest. In a year-long storage study, terpenoid levels fell by more than half on average within four months (Milay et al., 2020).

Different labs, different numbers

The report itself depends on who ran it. Labs don’t all measure the same list of compounds, their limits of quantitation differ, and they don’t always agree. An analysis of Washington State’s testing records found that the cannabinoid content reported for flower differed systematically depending on which laboratory did the testing, and that the differences persisted after accounting for other explanations (Jikomes & Zoorob, 2018). That study looked at cannabinoids rather than terpenes, but the lesson carries over: when you compare two reports, notice who produced each one.

What to trust instead

The lab report for the batch in your hand is the only document that describes that jar, and even it is a snapshot from test day. So:

  • Keep the report, not just the name. If you loved a jar, save its batch number and COA. NOSE saves the measured profile and shows the strain name only as printed, for reference.
  • Compare profiles, not names. When a favorite name comes back different, score the new batch against the old one rather than assuming.
  • Check the date. A report from months ago may describe a louder jar than the one you’re opening. How to read a COA covers what to look for.
